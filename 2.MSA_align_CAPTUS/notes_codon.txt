Great — I now have a thorough picture of all the relevant tools. Here's a clear breakdown of your situation:

---

## Do you need MACSE if Captus already aligned your .fna files?

**Probably not — but it depends on your data quality and downstream goals.** Here's the reasoning:

### What Captus already does
When aligning coding sequences, Captus codon-aligns the nucleotide version using the amino acid alignment of the locus as a template. So your `.fna` files from Captus are already codon-aware alignments in FASTA format — the core thing MACSE is meant to provide.

### What MACSE uniquely adds
MACSE's key distinction is that it can align coding sequences even when they contain frameshifts and/or premature stop codons — situations where standard AA-guided approaches break down, such as with pseudogenes, assembly errors, or non-model sequencing contigs.

If your Captus assemblies are clean, well-extracted coding sequences from good-quality data, you are unlikely to benefit from re-running MACSE. MACSE becomes valuable when your sequences have assembly errors, frameshifts, or you suspect pseudogenes in the dataset.

---

## Does GERP need codon-aware alignment?

**GERP does not require it.** GERP scores are calculated without using or modelling the codon structure of protein-coding genes — it operates purely on nucleotide-level substitution rates to identify constrained sites. A well-trimmed nucleotide alignment (like Captus produces) is sufficient. The key requirement for GERP is a *correctly aligned*, *multi-species* nucleotide alignment paired with a species tree.

---

## Does HyPhy need codon-aware alignment?

**Yes — this is where alignment quality really matters.** HyPhy requires a multiple alignment of homologous coding nucleotide sequences, and its codon-based models (dN/dS, BUSTED, FEL, MEME, etc.) all assume that the reading frame is preserved across the alignment. If gaps are not in multiples of three, or if the reading frame is disrupted, the results will be meaningless.

HyPhy accepts input in FASTA, PHYLIP, or Nexus format. So your `.fna` files (which are FASTA) are directly compatible — no reformatting needed.

Notably, HyPhy even provides its own `codon-msa` utility that can perform frameshift correction and translate sequences to amino acids for alignment, as an alternative to MACSE for pre-processing.

---

## Practical recommendation

| Tool | Needs codon-aware alignment? | Captus .fna sufficient? |
|---|---|---|
| **GERP** | No | ✅ Yes |
| **HyPhy** | Yes (reading frame must be intact) | ✅ Yes, if Captus output is clean |

**You can likely proceed directly to GERP and HyPhy with your Captus `.fna` files.** However, before running HyPhy, it's worth doing a quick sanity check:

1. Verify alignment length is divisible by 3 for each locus.
2. Check for premature stop codons in translations (you can use a tool like `seqkit translate` or HyPhy's own pre-processing script).
3. If you find frequent frameshifts or stop codons, *then* consider running MACSE's OMM_MACSE pipeline on those specific loci.

MACSE would be most warranted if your taxa include degraded sequences (e.g., from herbarium specimens, ancient DNA, or transcriptome-derived contigs with assembly errors).
